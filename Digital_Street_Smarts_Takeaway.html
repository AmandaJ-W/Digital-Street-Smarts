Lesson 1: Scam Emails, Calls & Texts

Every day, millions of people receive messages designed to trick them. Scammers reach out by email, phone, and text — often pretending to be your bank, a government agency, a delivery company, or a family member in trouble. Their goal: get you to act quickly before you have time to think.

🚩 Email Red Flags to Watch For
  • Urgent or threatening subject lines — "Act now!" / "Your account will be deleted"
  • Sender email doesn't match the company (e.g. support@paypa1.com instead of paypal.com)
  • Generic greetings like "Dear Customer" instead of your name
  • Spelling mistakes, odd symbols, or awkward grammar
  • Suspicious links or unexpected file attachments
  • Requests for payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency
✅ The Hover-before-you-click technique

On a computer: Move your mouse over a link without clicking. The real web address will appear in the bottom-left corner of your screen. Check it carefully before clicking.

On a phone or tablet: Press and hold the link until a preview appears. Slide your finger away — then lift — to avoid accidentally opening it.

The safest option: Type the official website address yourself in your browser rather than clicking any link.

Common scam types at a glance

Scam typeHow it worksYour defense
Grandchild scam Pretends to be a family member in urgent trouble — accident, arrest, stranded abroad Hang up. Call that family member directly on a number you already have saved.
Government impersonation Fake IRS, Social Security, or Medicare claims — threatening arrest or benefit loss Hang up. Real agencies send letters by mail first. Call their official number.
Tech support scam Fake Microsoft/Apple alert; asks you to install software or pay to "fix" a virus Do not call the number. Real companies do not contact you this way.
Phishing email Fake bank or store email with a link to a lookalike website to steal your login Do not click. Type the company's address yourself in your browser.
🚨 No legitimate business or government agency will EVER ask you to pay using a gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency. If they do — it is a scam, 100% of the time.

Lesson 2: Websites

Getting you to click a link is only the first step. Once you land on a fake website, scammers need you to trust it enough to enter your personal or financial information. Fraudulent sites copy the logos, colors, and layouts of real companies. A website can look completely real and still be fake.

🌐 Reading a web address (URL)

A web address (URL) has two key parts to check:

  • https:// — the "S" means the connection is encrypted (secure). If it only says "http://" (no S), leave immediately.
  • The domain name — e.g. amazon.com — look for misspellings or unusual extensions.

⚠️ Watch for tricks like:

  • Replacing letters with numbers: paypa1.com instead of paypal.com
  • Extra words added: amazon-support-help.com instead of amazon.com
  • Wrong extension: irs.net instead of irs.gov

💡 The padlock icon (🔒) in your browser means the connection is encrypted — but scammers can have it too. Always also check the domain name.

Spotting a fake online store

Warning signWhat to look for
🏷️ Extreme discounts70–90% off luxury or designer goods is almost certainly a scam
📷 Stolen photosSame photo on multiple sites with different brand names
⭐ No reviewsNo feedback history, or all reviews posted on the same day
📞 No contact infoNo phone number, address, or verifiable email on the site
💳 Unusual paymentOnly accepts gift cards, cryptocurrency, or wire transfers
✅ Website safety checklist — Ask yourself before entering any information
  • ✅ Did I arrive here by typing the address myself (not by clicking a link)?
  • ✅ Does the address start with https://?
  • ✅ Is the domain name spelled correctly?
  • ✅ Does this site have real contact information?
  • ✅ Have I searched the site name + "scam" to check its reputation?
The golden rule: NEVER enter personal or financial information on a website until you have verified it is legitimate.

Lesson 3: AI-generated content & deepfakes

Artificial Intelligence (AI) can now create realistic fake photos, clone voices from just a few seconds of audio, and generate convincing videos of real people saying things they never said. These are called deepfakes. You do not need to spot every fake — you just need a healthy sense of caution and the habit of verifying before acting.

👀 Visual clues in photos & videos
  • 👐 Hands and fingers look twisted, missing, or have too many digits
  • 👁️ Blinking is too frequent, too rare, or unnatural
  • 👄 Lip sync is slightly off — words don't quite match the mouth movements
  • 🌫️ Edges around hair, face, or background are blurry or smudged
  • 💡 Lighting and shadows look uneven or fall in the wrong direction
  • 🔄 Inconsistencies — hair color, clothing, or skin tone changes mid-video
  • 📏 Very short — AI deepfake videos are often only a few seconds long
  • 🔤 Text in images looks distorted or doesn't make sense
👂 Audio clues in voice messages
  • 🎵 Unnaturally smooth or flat tone — no emotion or variation in the voice
  • 💨 No breathing sounds — real speech includes natural pauses and breaths
  • ⏱️ Strange pacing — words come too quickly, too evenly, or with odd pauses
  • 🗣️ Mispronunciations — certain words sound slightly off or oddly emphasized

Common AI scam scenarios

AI scam typeHow it worksYour defense
Voice cloning Uses a few seconds of a real person's voice to fake a call from a loved one in trouble Hang up. Call that person directly on a number you already have.
Fake celebrity endorsements AI video of a celebrity promoting a fake investment or product Real celebrities don't endorse products in unsolicited videos. Ignore it.
Romance scams Fake profile with AI-generated photo builds a relationship, then asks for money Never send money to someone you haven't met in person.
If a video, photo, or voice message feels unusual — or asks you to do something urgently or provide personal information — stop and verify through a separate channel before taking any action.

Lesson 4: Your Emergency Toolbox

Scams can happen to anyone, even when you are being careful. What matters most is knowing what to do in the moment. This toolbox gives you a clear, simple plan to stay calm, take control, and protect yourself.

STOP 🛑
VERIFY 🔎
REPORT 📝
BLOCK 🚫

🛑 STOP

  • Do not respond right away
  • Do not click links or open attachments
  • Do not share any personal information
  • If you are on a call — hang up

🔎 VERIFY

  • Call your bank using the number on your card
  • Contact the person directly on a number you already have saved
  • Visit official websites by typing the address yourself
  • Never use contact details provided in the suspicious message

📝 REPORT

  • Report to the FTC: reportfraud.ftc.gov or call 1-877-382-4357
  • Report to the FBI: ic3.gov
  • Forward suspicious texts to 7726 (SPAM)
  • Use the "Report Spam" or "Report Phishing" option in your email

🚫 BLOCK & DELETE

  • Block the phone number or sender
  • Delete the message
  • Do not reply — even to messages that say "STOP" or "UNSUBSCRIBE"

If you think you've already been scammed

What happenedWhat to do right away
Shared a password Change it immediately. Update any account that uses the same password.
Shared bank or card info Call your bank right away. Ask them to freeze your card and monitor your account.
Shared your Social Security Number Place a credit freeze with Equifax (1-800-525-6285), Experian (1-888-397-3742), and TransUnion (1-800-680-7289). Visit identitytheft.gov.
Clicked a suspicious link Close the page. Run a virus scan on your device. Change your passwords.
Gave remote access to your device Disconnect from internet. Turn off device. Change passwords from another device. Contact your bank.

🧠 Your #1 Defense

Scammers rely on panic, urgency, and trust. Your best protection against all of them is the same simple habit: pause before you act. Slow down, look closely, and when in doubt, verify through a source you already know and trust. You are not alone — help is always available.
